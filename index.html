A word can hide an act

Every source in this file was made by someone, for a reason. Some words soften what happened. Some leave people out. Some tell you who belongs, or who to blame, or what you should do next.

The Bias Button asks the same four questions of every source you open:

  1. Who made it, and why?
  2. Who is missing?
  3. What are the loaded words doing?
  4. What does it want you to think or do?

The button does not give the answer. “We can’t know” is a fair finding when a source will not support a firm claim.

Here is a real line, printed in The Sydney Morning Herald on 20 January 1912. A traveller is talking about a police party sent out in Western Australia, and about a telegram that later appeared in the papers:

A telegram in the papers

The Sydney Morning Herald · 20 January 1912 · “Out Back”

This source uses the language of its time, including the word “natives,” and it is about police violence. We are reading it to see what the words are doing.

“when I got down to Perth I saw a telegram in the papers that his party had dispersed 14 natives, and you know what dispersed means.”

Source. “Out Back,” The Sydney Morning Herald, 20 January 1912. National Library of Australia, Trove.

What did “dispersed” stand for in that sentence? Who is not speaking? The Case has four investigations. The first two are about racism in historical sources, which is the core of this file. 1. The word that hides the act

Some official and newspaper words sound milder than the act they describe. Read these three sources, then write a finding.

Read

The Moreton Bay Courier, 24 April 1858

Newspaper report · “Fatal Encounter, with the Blacks. Attack on the Native Police.”

This source uses racist and violent language from its time, and it leaves people out. We are reading it to see what the words are doing, not to repeat the harm. You can stop and talk with your teacher.

The paper says Lieutenant Williams and his men “succeeded in utterly routing and dispersing the blacks,” that “the actual loss of the blacks is not known,” and that “the general forbearance of the troopers is construed by the ignorant savages as an evidence of cowardice.” It also speaks of “a severe chastisement.”

Source. The Moreton Bay Courier, 24 April 1858. National Library of Australia, Trove. Short excerpt only; open the article for the full report.

A modern comparison: “illegal arrivals,” 2013

ABC News · 22 October 2013 · report by Emma Griffiths

This card is about word choice in a published news report. It is not a verdict on a living person. Quote only the published lines.

Immigration Minister Scott Morrison is quoted saying he is “calling a spade a spade,” that he has “never claimed that it’s illegal to claim asylum,” and that the term “refers to their mode of entry.” Critics in the same report say the language “seeks to demonise people who could have genuine claims to refugee status.”

2. Who is allowed in?

A law can draw a line without using the word “race” on its face. A magazine can say the line out loud. Read a 1901 debate extract, the Act, and a later masthead, then compare them with the 2013 reports.

Read

Kalgoorlie Western Argus, 22 October 1901

Newspaper extract from Federal Hansard · Immigration Restriction Bill

This debate is about keeping people out of Australia on racial lines, including an explicit colour bar moved in the House.

The paper prints “an extract from the ‘Federal Hansard’” of the House of Representatives debate. It sets out the proposed dictation test: a person is prohibited if they fail to “write out and sign in the presence of the officer a passage of 50 words in length in the English language dictated by the officer.” J. C. Watson (Bland) moves an explicit colour bar instead: “Any person who is an aboriginal native of Asia, Africa, or of the islands thereof.” Other speakers talk of “a ‘white Australia.’”

Source. “Immigration Restriction Bill,” Kalgoorlie Western Argus, 22 October 1901. National Library of Australia, Trove. Compare this printed debate with the official as-made Act (Immigration Restriction Act 1901, No. 17).

3. Send a man

Wartime posters and later articles can tell women what they are for, and what they should give up. The same button works on a poster, a feature article, and a modern ad.

Read

To the women of Australia, 1915

First World War recruitment poster · Australian War Memorial ARTV02512

To the women of Australia… won’t you help and send a man to the front today?

Typographic card of the catalogue title, not a shock crop of the poster. Open the Memorial’s page for the full item, which is in the public domain.

The Memorial’s catalogue describes a 1915 Victorian Government Printer poster that puts four questions to women and urges them to send “lovers, brothers and friends” to enlist, referring to alleged German atrocities in Belgium.

Source. Australian War Memorial, accession ARTV02512, “To the women of Australia...won't you help and send a man to the front today?” Public domain. Catalogue also at C98067.

The Daily Telegraph, 31 January 1938

Sydney feature · “Business Jobs for Women--But not Married Ones”

This article tells married women they cannot compete in business. We are reading it to see what the words are doing.

A market-research executive is reported as saying that “married women cannot run a home efficiently, care for a husband and perhaps children, and then compete successfully with single women in business efficiency,” and that “it is the single woman … the single woman only” who has built women’s reputation in business.

4. Who gets the label?

Class bias often arrives as a short, sticky word. Read a 1931 letter and a 1976 report, then test a current headline.

Read

The Murrumbidgee Irrigator, 30 January 1931

Letter to the editor · “Getting Rations” · signed J. O’Shannessy, Leeton, 29 January 1931

The writer says people who “have to go to the Police Station for the ration dole, are subject to the most humiliating enquiries,” and describes a young man “turned over like a common ‘dip’” (searched), then insulted over a shilling in his pocket.

Source. J. O’Shannessy, “Getting Rations,” The Murrumbidgee Irrigator, 30 January 1931. National Library of Australia, Trove.

The marriage bar

Official context on the Commonwealth marriage bar: it required women in the Commonwealth Public Service to resign when they married, and was abolished in 1966. Use these pages as context, then return to the 1938 article with the Bias Button.

  • Parliamentary Education Office, “Marriage bar abolished”: peo.gov.au
  • National Archives of Australia learning resource, “Appointment of women to the Commonwealth Public Service – Note for Cabinet discussion”: naa.gov.au

An Education Queensland historical survey quotes a 1902 departmental regulation, “Female teachers shall resign when they marry,” later elaborated so that a female teacher who did not resign “shall be deemed to have forfeited her office.” This is a later official history quoting the rule, not the 1902 gazette itself.

Source. education.qld.gov.au PDF, 1902–1940.

Teacher notes

Teacher Note

Student screens. This file carries no year level and no author’s name on the student pages. If you need a year-level judgement, the routine will likely suit secondary history and civics, as a source-analysis skill rather than a single content strand. That decision is for the teacher.

What’s true on The Page. The opener is a frame, not an invented diary or a made-up document. The 1912 Sydney Morning Herald sentence is a real, cited extract. The four Bias Button questions are the routine of this file.

Harmful language. A warning sits above sources that use racist, sexist or violent language, or that describe police violence or wartime atrocity claims. Racial slurs and long violent detail stay in the linked archive; the app quotes only a short excerpt. Offer students a way to step out. Do not ask students to role-play a racist voice, or to retell ceremony or sacred material.

Cultural safety. Only openly shareable public-record content is used. There is no secret or sacred material, no ceremony, no retold Dreaming stories, no rock art, and no photographs of deceased Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander people. Where a person is shown, a silhouette is used, not a likeness. The 1858 report names the Ningi-ningi and gives no Aboriginal account; that absence is part of what students are asked to notice. Teach with AIATSIS guidance and local community protocols where First Nations histories are discussed.

Past racist publications. The Bulletin masthead is a typographic treatment of a documented slogan, with a link to the National Library issue. Do not host or project the cartoons in class unless you have previewed the issue and judged it appropriate. The Teacher Note, not the student screen, is the place for any longer discussion of that publication.

Living people. The 2013 ABC and Herald reports quote a then minister and his critics. Stay with the published wording. The task is to read the language, not to attack a person.

Barton’s 1901 speech, for teachers only. This file does not print a long extract on the student screen. The Parliamentary Library briefing “The immigration debate in Australia: from Federation to World War One” quotes Edmund Barton from the House of Representatives debate of 26 September 1901, p. 5233, including “There is no racial equality.” An archived copy of that official briefing is at this web.archive.org copy of the Parliamentary Library paper. Check the official Hansard before teaching any sentence as a confirmed quotation from the floor. A direct ParlInfo fetch was not relied on in the planning of this file.

The 1861 report. Students are sent to the digitised minutes, not given an unverified quote as if it were copied from that volume here.

Modern classroom sources. For the advertisement and the current headline, prefer ordinary published items. If an example names a private person, do not use it.

Pictures and links. The QR code uses a button emblem, not a likeness. The War Memorial posters are linked, not copied into this file as shock images. Trove, legislation.gov.au, the National Library, foundingdocs.gov.au, peo.gov.au, naa.gov.au and awm.gov.au are the places for the full items.
